State of the Art on Nuclear Space Applications and Fuels in Nuclear Propulsion

Download report (PDF)

A literature review of nuclear propulsion systems for space applications, covering both the reactor architectures and the fuel materials that make them possible. Nuclear propulsion has long been seen as a way to enable missions that chemical propulsion cannot reach: for the same propellant mass, a nuclear fission source carries roughly seven orders of magnitude more energy density than a chemical propellant, which is why nuclear rocket programs in the US and USSR demonstrated engines twice as efficient as the best chemical engines of the time, even though none were ultimately flown.

The review first distinguishes the two main families of nuclear propulsion. Nuclear Electric Propulsion (NEP) uses a reactor to generate electricity, which then ionizes and accelerates a propellant (typically hydrogen, lithium, or xenon) through an electric or magnetic field, producing low thrust at very high efficiency. Nuclear Thermal Propulsion (NTP) instead pumps liquid hydrogen directly through the reactor core, where it heats rapidly and expands through a nozzle, producing an order of magnitude more thrust than NEP. Today's best chemical engines reach a specific impulse of about 465 s; NTP can reach roughly 900 s, nearly double, while also offering a high thrust-to-weight ratio that would meaningfully reduce the number of launches needed for a crewed Mars mission and shorten transit time, in turn reducing crew exposure to radiation and microgravity.

The most mature NTP architecture is the solid core reactor, as used in NASA's NERVA program: hexagonal fuel elements are packed together to form the reactor core, with hydrogen flowing through channels in each element to cool the reactor while being heated for thrust. A solid core reactor of this type typically reaches a specific impulse around 800 s at a thrust-to-weight ratio of 3–12, roughly twice the specific impulse of the best chemical engines.

Hydrogen is the propellant of choice for NTP because its low molecular weight maximizes exhaust velocity for a given chamber temperature, and it doubles as an effective neutron moderator. That same property, however, is the source of the programme's hardest materials problem: at the high temperatures needed for good performance, hydrogen reacts with graphite fuel elements to form hydrocarbons, progressively corroding the fuel rods. The literature solution reviewed here layers a niobium carbide (or zirconium carbide) diffusion barrier over the fuel element, topped with a molybdenum overlayer: the NbC layer blocks carbon diffusion from the graphite substrate, while the ductile molybdenum layer seals any cracks that form during deposition and slows hydrogen diffusion further still.

The review also covers the fuel production routes that make these elements possible — converting UF6 to ceramic-grade UO2 powder, then carburizing or nitriding it into uranium carbide or uranium nitride fuel forms with the thermal conductivity and high-temperature stability NTP requires — alongside the environmental safeguards mandated for any space reactor, from remaining subcritical in the event of a launch accident to the disposal strategy for a spent reactor at end of life.

Finally, the review looks at where the field stands today: NASA and DARPA's DRACO program aims to demonstrate a nuclear thermal rocket in orbit, targeting a flight demonstration in the late 2020s, while ESA has launched parallel feasibility studies (RocketRoll for NEP, ALUMNI for NTP) to identify the technologies Europe would need to mature to develop nuclear propulsion domestically.
